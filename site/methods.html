Who lost each race

The headline test shows that a bot got to a prepared exploit transaction, or to an attacker's on-chain setup, before that transaction or setup paid out. It does not by itself show that the account the bot beat was the original attacker. So we checked every loser separately: its own preparation history (deployments, tests, earlier attempts), where its code came from, how it was funded, and what the victim's report or other write-ups say. The series is still called The Bot Beat the Hacker; in this series, “attacker” means the account behind the exploit transaction or setup that the bot beat.

The original attacker, confirmed
The loser's own setup trail (deployments, tests, earlier attempts in the same campaign) or the victim's report ties it to the attack the bot intercepted. This does not prove a human was behind it, or that no unseen private transaction existed.
Probably the original attacker
The loser prepared the attack itself, but where the plan came from is not fully traced: funding, code origin or authorship stay open.
A later exploiter, or we can't tell
The loser is a demonstrated follow-on exploiter (for example an account that had already copied the same exploit on another pool), or its link to the original attack is unresolved. It may have been another bot, a related account, or someone else; we do not say which.

These verdicts are about who lost. They do not change the copy-and-order evidence that put each race in the headline count. Before August 2023 they rest on in-block evidence only.

How the "raw" links work

Every timestamp and number on a race page carries a small raw tag. It opens the saved response the number was read from: an RPC block, a set of receipts, a block trace, a relay's bid stream, a payload-delivered record, or a mempool archive extract. Responses were saved when we fetched them, with a checksum and the source address, listed in raw/MANIFEST.json.

Where a number is derived (a delay, a share, a sum), the tag points at the response it was derived from and the arithmetic is shown. Where no single response backs a value, the tag says no raw instead of linking something unrelated.

Blocks that contain c0ffeebabe.eth's transactions are saved at block level only, with no per-transaction traces or decodes of those transactions, by publication policy. The mempool archive files are far too large to host. For those we publish only the rows that concern each incident (a .rows.csv extract), plus a pointer to the original day file. Saved third-party web pages use link-only stubs; MANIFEST notes describe every reduction and redaction. Absent rows are not evidence of absence: see the limits below.

Evidence levels

confirmed-onchain
The same or adapted calldata sent from a different address, in the same or an earlier block than the attacker's own transaction, shown in the traces. Or the bot used the attacker's setup (a deployed contract or a prepare() call) before the attacker's own call. For c0ffeebabe.eth rows our call-level comparison is withheld by publication policy; anyone can compare the public traces. Public transaction hashes, blocks and positions are listed on each page.
reported
A postmortem or press report says a bot did it, and we have not yet verified that on chain. Not counted in any headline.
related-not-frontrun
A bot or whitehat was involved, but not as a front-run of a pending attack: it replayed a failed attack, found the bug first, or copied a long time after.
rejected
Checked and not a race. The reason is given in the table. These rows are on the home page under "All races" because they are instructive: DeFiHackLabs often lists the bot's transaction as "the attack", and arbitrage bots touch the same pools by coincidence.

The headline count is the rows with verdict confirmed-onchain and race type beat a pending attack or used the attacker's setup. Euler is excluded because the copier may be the attacker’s own second account.

Before August 2023 there is no public mempool archive. For those races the lane that would show the mempool says "in-block evidence only" and the claim rests on block order and traces.

Builder bid response (ms)

Builder bid response (ms) measures the gap between the first public sighting of the trigger (the attacker's transaction, their contract deployment, or a user's withdrawal) and the first big step-up bid from the builder that went on to win the block. A step-up is a bid at least twice that builder's own best earlier bid and at least 0.5 ETH.

Bid contents are never visible, only the amount and the time. So the bid we pick might not contain the bot's transaction, and a bot that reacted earlier with a smaller bid would be missed. Where a second estimate exists we show it as an upper bound. The response can be timed only when the trigger was seen before inclusion.

Limits

  • Relay retention. In our fetches only the Flashbots relay returned old bid data; the others returned data for roughly the last 90 days. For older races, bid curves come from whichever relay still has them, and some slots have none.
  • Bids never reveal contents. We see an amount, a builder and a time. We cannot say what was in a losing bid.
  • The Flashbots blocks API is gone. It now returns HTTP 410 (“this website has been decommissioned”). Bundles from before the Merge are inferred from traces and labelled inferred.
  • The mempool archive is a sample. It starts on 2023-08-07 and aggregates several sources whose coverage drifts: it saw 89% of ordinary transactions in 2023 Q3 and about half in 2025 and 2026. A transaction absent from the archive is not seen, which can reflect private flow or a missed observation. That is why every exposure number is shown beside its control group.
  • Sample of exploits. The evolution study covers reproduced public incidents on Ethereum mainnet, not all attacks.
  • Yoink’s executor is decoded from its on-chain code; other operators are described through their public transactions. We do not analyse the internals of one bot, by decision. The Yoink explainer describes that operator’s public transaction behaviour.
  • Money afterwards comes from on-chain transfers we could trace plus public statements. “No return found” means we found none.

Prior work

Four works measured parts of this problem. Our contribution is different in kind: per-race reconstructions from relay bid streams and traces, rather than a system or a prevalence estimate.

Return status

Returned means everything went back to the victim side except a bounty the victim agreed to or offered. Partial means some came back, otherwise; none means known zero within the documented search window; unknown means unresolved. Priced pooled returns include known zeros.
