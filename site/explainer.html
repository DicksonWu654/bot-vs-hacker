How Yoink copied a Ronin bridge payout in one block

On 6 August 2024 a user asked the Ronin bridge to pay out about 2,641 USDC. That morning a broken check in the bridge meant a copy of the request could ask for far more. Seconds after the request showed up in public, a bot called Yoink had copied it, changed 3 of its 61 words, and collected just under 2 million USDC instead.

This is that bot, taken apart: what it watched, what it copied, what it paid to go first, and what it gave back.

What you need to know first

Every Ethereum transaction waits its turn to be written into a block. A bot watches the public queue, simulates a copy, and pays a builder to put its copy into the block before the original. Nine terms carry the whole story.

The machine in ten steps

Each step below uses the same race, the Ronin copy, with the real number from that day. Pick a step to read about it, or walk through them in order.

    Step 1 of 10

    Watch for anything that pays

    A bot has to notice money it could take before anyone else does. That can be a request still waiting in the public mempool, a setup transaction that just landed, or a contract that was just deployed.

    Step 2 of 10

    Run it in private first

    Before copying anything, a bot must know what a transaction does. It runs it on its own copy of the chain and reads off who ends up richer.

    That morning an upgrade had broken a check in the Ronin bridge, so a withdrawal request with a different recipient, or a bigger amount, would still pay. We replayed the same tests on a local copy of the chain. Nothing was broadcast.

    Step 3 of 10

    Copy it, and change who gets paid

    The user's request is 61 words of 32 bytes each. Yoink's copy kept 58 of them, including the 16 validator signatures that authorize this bridge payout.

    It changed only the two recipient addresses, to its own contract, and the amount.

    Step 4 of 10

    Wrap the copy in a small program

    Yoink's contract has no fixed strategy. Calls to its main entry carry a little program: a header, then a list of operations run in order.

    The copied request is just one operation. The rest turn the haul into ETH or send it to the bot's vault. At Ronin the program ran in this order:

    1. submit the copied bridge withdrawal
    2. swap the USDC for WETH
    3. unwrap the WETH into ETH
    4. check the profit (step 5)
    5. pay the builder (step 6)

    Step 5 of 10

    Refuse to lose money

    The contract notes its balance before the program runs and again after. If the gain is smaller than a minimum written in the header, the whole transaction undoes itself with the message poor.

    If the check fails, everything the transaction did is rolled back. Only the gas is still spent.

    Step 6 of 10

    Hand most of the profit to the block builder

    At Ronin, Yoink handed 99.9% of the profit to whoever built the block, as the payment for going first. Only the later refund in step 9 made the place cheap.

    It sets that payment in the value field of its own call. Below 1,000 the value is a share of the profit in thousandths; at Ronin it was 999. From 1,000 up it is a fixed amount. Try the rule yourself; the presets are real transactions.

    Step 7 of 10

    Win the auction for the block

    A payment to a builder matters only if that builder's block is the one the proposer publishes. Builders bid for each 12-second slot through relays, and the proposer picks one, normally the highest.

    Step 8 of 10

    Get in line ahead of the original

    Order inside a block decides who is paid. Yoink's copy ran near the top of its block. The user's own request came one block later and failed, because that withdrawal had already been paid.

    Loading the blocks…

    Step 9 of 10

    Pay the builder, then get most of it back

    Inside the one transaction the USDC became ETH and the bribe went to the block's builder. Later in the same block the builder sent most of it straight back to Yoink's contract and paid the proposer the winning bid.

    The refund pattern links the builder and Yoink, and so do the builder's name and EigenPhi's labelling, but the chain does not prove that they share an operator.

    Loading the block's payments…

    Step 10 of 10

    Sometimes, give it back

    Returning the money was a choice made after winning, not part of the protocol's ordering rules. At Ronin, Yoink moved most of the proceeds before the team asked for a return. That afternoon most of it reached Ronin's multisignature Safe wallet.

    Across Yoink's races in our data the answer varies, from almost everything returned to nothing found.

    The takeaway

    Taking the Ronin money took three changed words and a place near the top of one block, bought with almost all of the profit. Giving it back was up to the bot.

    What we cannot see

    The evidence is on-chain behaviour, saved relay and mempool responses, and our own local replays.

    • The off-chain half. How Yoink finds targets, how it simulates and how it picks the bribe all happen on its own servers. Steps 1 and 2 describe what any bot must do; the chain only shows the result.
    • Some names are ours. The names of the swap operations (2 to 5) are inferred from what they do, and so is the reading that the Ronin amount came from a search for the largest payable amount.
    • "First seen" is the archive's clock. It is when the Flashbots mempool archive's sources saw a transaction, not when Yoink did.
    • Builder and bot as one operator is inferred from in-block refunds, the builder name and EigenPhi's statement. No operator statement or on-chain label confirms it.
