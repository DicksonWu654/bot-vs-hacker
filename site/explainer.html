How a front-running bot beats a hacker

One toy exploit, followed through eight steps: a public withdraw(to) function that pays whoever the caller names. Then the same story with a real bot, Yoink, taken apart from its on-chain behaviour.

Evidence labels follow the project: confirmed on chain unless marked inferred. Pre-August-2023 claims are in-block evidence only.

Step 1 of 8

The attacker prepares, usually in two transactions

Most exploits are split in two: deploy an exploit contract, then trigger it. In BACKRUNNER's sample of 54 attacks (January 2023 to May 2024), 50 had the exploit contract deployed at least a second before the trigger. That gap is the bot's window.

Some attacks do everything inside a contract's constructor, so no separate trigger exists to copy. Exploit contracts also tend to carry their own guards: an equality check on msg.sender or tx.origin against a constant is the most common.

Step 2 of 8

The transaction travels in public, or in private

A public transaction is broadcast over the peer-to-peer network, so anyone running a node sees it. A private one goes straight to a builder or a searcher-facing relay and stays invisible until it is included.

BACKRUNNER (a different method, a different sample) measured 158 attack transactions between January 2023 and May 2024 and found only 17 in the public mempool before block broadcast. In our own measurement, most exploit transactions never touched the public mempool.

Step 3 of 8

The bot sees it, and throws most things away

A generalized front-runner watches pending transactions. Nearly all are not worth a simulation, so a cheap filter runs first. Real deployments read from low-latency feeds rather than their own gossip.

We have not measured the bot's funnel, so the diagram is qualitative: thousands in, a handful simulated.

Step 4 of 8

Simulate on a fork and find who gains

The bot runs the pending transaction against a local fork of the latest state. If it reverts, give up. If it succeeds, look for an account whose balance goes up. If no one gains, give up.

The original Dark Forest essay added a nastier detail: a bot can execute the transaction and copy profitable internal calls from its trace, which defeated simple obfuscation.

Step 5 of 8

Rewrite it so the profit lands with the bot

The naive version replaces the victim's address in the calldata with the bot's. It works when the profit goes to the sender, or to an address the calldata names. Where the attack runs through the attacker's own contract, a bot has to rebuild that contract with its own addresses swapped in.

Guards matter. If the copy reverts on a CALLER == constant check, a capable bot patches the comparison and redeploys. And some attacks cannot be copied at all: constructor-only exploits, private-only ones, hash-gated ones.

Step 6 of 8

Decide how much to bid, and where to send it

Era by era the mechanism changed. Before 2021, bots fought public gas auctions. From December 2020, Flashbots bundles let searchers pay a miner privately, by gas price or a direct coinbase transfer. Since the Merge the bundle goes to a builder, who includes it and bids for the slot.

The cost of winning is large. In the races on this site the bot's coinbase payment is often most of the loot: see the "to builder" column.

Step 7 of 8

The builder auction inside the 12-second slot

Builders send bids to relays. The relay forwards the best to the proposer, who signs a header, and only then is the block released. A builder's later bid can replace its earlier one. The auction is effectively over when the proposer signs, not when anyone asks for a header.

That is why the replays on this site draw bids against time relative to slot start, and why a bid that arrives a second late can be higher and still lose.

Step 8 of 8

Afterwards: who owns the money?

Nothing in the protocol decides. In practice it is ad hoc: on-chain messages, bounty offers of around 10%, sometimes nothing. The SEAL Safe Harbor (a pre-agreed rescue framework) sets a 72-hour return window and a bounty live overview (archive lookup); as far as we read it, it does not address MEV bots. We found no documented case of a bot refusing to return and being pursued, and say "we found none", not "none exist".

Yoink is the bot we could take apart furthest: at Ronin we can put a user's calldata and the bot's copy side by side. It has three generations of contract and an input format that decodes exactly. 3. Simulate, then the profit guard

The contract takes a balance snapshot before and after the script and reverts with the string poor unless the balance rose by at least a caller-chosen minimum x. A bad guess costs gas, not capital. That is also why gen 2 and gen 3 revert so rarely: the off-chain simulation filters first.

profit = S1 - S0
require(S1 >= S0 + x)  else revert "poor"

4. A per-mille bribe to the block's coinbase

The call's msg.value is not a payment. It is a parameter that the contract turns into a coinbase transfer. Below 1,000 it is a per-mille share of the profit; at 1,000 or more it is a fixed amount.

msg.value == 0:     no payment
msg.value  < 1000:  bribe = profit * v / 1000;  if bribe > profit - x then bribe = (profit - x) / 2
msg.value >= 1000:  bribe = v * 1e11 wei

5. The builder refund round trip

The bot pays nearly all of its profit to the block's fee recipient. In two of the three blocks below the builder then sends part of it straight back to the bot's contract in the same block, and at Ronin nearly all of it. The proposer gets only the winning bid.

Who operates the builder behind these blocks is not proven by chain data alone. EigenPhi's labelling names it as Yoink's; we report that and have not verified it on chain.

What is not known

  • The off-chain logic: how it finds targets, how it simulates, how it chooses v.
  • The names of ops 2 to 5, and the explanation that the Ronin quantity came from bisection (2,000,000 x (1 - 2-10)). Both are inferences.
  • Gen 1 was only sampled and not decoded further.
  • "First seen" is the mempool archive's observation, not the bot's.
